Concrete work runs on thin margins. On a typical foundation and slab package, a missed handful of cubic yards, an under-counted rebar lap or a forgotten pump charge can be the difference between a profitable job and one you work for free.

Most of these misses aren't big scope gaps. They're small items that hide between the drawings, the specifications and the way the work actually gets built in the field. This checklist covers the 12 we see most often when reviewing concrete takeoffs, grouped by where they show up.

## Why small concrete misses add up

Estimating software is very good at measuring what's drawn. It's less good at accounting for what isn't: the trench that caves in, the bar that has to overlap, the line that has to be primed before the first yard comes out of the pump.

Each item below may only be a few hundred or a few thousand dollars on its own. Together, on a mid-size commercial job, they routinely add up to more than the profit you carried.

## 1. Footings, stepped elevations and over-excavation

Footings are drawn with crisp 90-degree edges, but they are rarely dug that way. Excavators work with a bucket, trench walls slough and soft spots get over-dug and filled with concrete. Where footings are poured against earth instead of formed, every one of those voids fills with ready-mix.

Stepped footings add another layer. Each step needs its own form, its own volume calculation and often extra dowels. If your takeoff treats a stepped footing as one long rectangle, both the yardage and the formwork will be low.

<ProTip title="Read the geotechnical report first">
The soils report tells you whether over-excavation and engineered fill are required under footings, and how deep. If the engineer calls for over-excavation, budget the extra concrete or fill **before** you price the footing, and use a higher waste factor on earth-formed trench footings than on formed walls.
</ProTip>

## 2. Rebar lap splices and bundled bars

The fastest way to lose money on reinforcing is to measure centerline lengths, divide by stock bar length and stop there. Continuous bars have to overlap at every splice, and the structural general notes tell you how much. Laps are usually specified as a multiple of the bar diameter, so larger bars need much longer laps.

Across a long perimeter foundation wall with several horizontal bars, the laps alone can add a meaningful percentage to the total weight. Corner bars, hooks, dowels into slabs and bundled bars at beams all add more.

<CommonMistake title="Assuming stock lengths produce no scrap">
Stock bars rarely cut evenly into the lengths a foundation needs. Steps, returns, openings and dowel tails all create offcuts that can't be reused. Carry a cut-off allowance on top of the laps unless the reinforcing is fully detailed and fabricated to length.
</CommonMistake>

## Field checklist: items 3 through 8

These are the items that most often disappear between the plan view and the field.

<ChecklistItem number="03" title="Bulkheads, keyways and chamfers">
Automated takeoffs trace perimeter forms well but often miss construction joint bulkheads, keyed cold joints and chamfer strip. Every planned pour break needs a bulkhead, and much of that lumber can't be reused.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="04" title="Vapor retarder laps and penetrations">
Under-slab vapor retarders need taped, overlapping seams and sealed boots at every plumbing and electrical penetration. Measure the slab area, then add a waste factor for laps and an allowance for boots and tape.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="05" title="Pump primer and washout">
Every pump setup needs line primer, and most jurisdictions require contained washout. Count the number of pours, not just the total yardage, because these costs are per placement.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="06" title="Dowel baskets and column isolation">
Slab control joints often call for dowel baskets, and column block-outs need isolation joint material. They're small line items that are easy to forget and add up over a large slab.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="07" title="Thickened edges and slab depressions">
Thickened slab edges, depressed slabs at showers or freezers and sloped slabs to drains all change the volume. A slab measured at one uniform thickness will be light.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="08" title="Embeds, anchor bolts and sleeves">
Embed plates, anchor bolts, sleeves and blockouts are often shown on different sheets from the concrete. Count them, and include the labor to set and check them before the pour.
</ChecklistItem>

## Waste factor benchmarks to check

Waste factors depend on the element, the subgrade and how the work is formed. The ranges below are common starting points for checking an estimate, not rules. Adjust them for your crews, your suppliers and the site.

| Item | Unit | Typical waste range | Why it varies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Formed walls and grade beams | CY | 3% – 5% | Form accuracy, pump line residue |
| Earth-formed trench footings | CY | 5% – 10% | Over-excavation, sloughing trench walls |
| Slabs on grade | CY | 3% – 8% | Subgrade tolerance, thickened edges |
| Reinforcing steel | LB | 5% – 10% | Laps, hooks, offcuts not shown on plans |
| Under-slab vapor retarder | SF | 10% – 15% | Seam laps, penetrations, edges |

## The last four items: general conditions you can't skip

<ChecklistItem number="09" title="Testing and inspection">
Specifications often require cylinders, slump tests or special inspection on every pour. If the owner isn't paying for testing directly, it belongs in your price.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="10" title="Cold and hot weather protection">
Blankets, heaters, enclosures, set retarders and accelerators all cost money, and the schedule decides which you'll need. Check when the concrete will actually be placed.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="11" title="Curing and finishing requirements">
Curing compounds, wet curing, sealers, hardeners and special finishes are usually in the specifications, not the drawings. Read Division 03 of the spec book, not just the plans.
</ChecklistItem>

<ChecklistItem number="12" title="Short loads and minimum charges">
Small pours like pads, curbs and infill often trigger short-load fees from ready-mix suppliers. Group them where you can and carry the fees where you can't.
</ChecklistItem>

## Protecting your concrete margin

The difference between winning a job and winning a profitable job is usually found in these details. Before you submit, check your takeoff against this list: over-excavation, laps and offcuts, bulkheads, vapor retarder laps, pump and washout costs, embeds, testing, weather protection, curing and short loads.
